Tonight Jeremiah is sent to preach at the one address where no one expects a rebuke - the gate of the temple itself, to people on their way in to worship.

A building treated as a charm, chanted three times over.

A ruin at Shiloh that nobody wanted to go look at.

A prophet who wishes his head were water so he could weep without stopping.

Jeremiah 7 is the temple sermon: stand where they enter, and tell them the address does not save them. Amend your ways. Do justice at the gate. And go see what happened to the last place I put My name.

Jeremiah 8 watches a people who will not turn - who know less about Yahweh's seasons than migrating birds know about their own - and ends with the question that has haunted every honest reader since: is there no balm in Gilead?

Jeremiah 9 is the weeping. Not restrained, not tidy - a call for professional mourners because the grief has outrun the ordinary means of expressing it. And in the middle of it, the verse that resets everything worth boasting about.

This is not just history.

This is a mirror.

And tonight, we let the Word fill us.

CHAPTER 7 - The Temple Sermon and the Valley of Slaughter

Key Notes

  • Jeremiah is stationed at the gate of Yahweh's house and told to preach to worshippers as they enter - the most confrontational possible pulpit (Jeremiah 7:1-2).
  • The offer is conditional and concrete: amend your ways and your doings, and I will cause you to dwell in this place (Jeremiah 7:3).
  • The threefold chant - Yahweh's temple, Yahweh's temple, Yahweh's temple - is named as lying words. Repetition had turned a promise into a superstition (Jeremiah 7:4, 8).
  • What amending actually means is spelled out in social terms: justice between neighbors, no oppression of the foreigner, the fatherless, or the widow, no innocent blood, no other gods (Jeremiah 7:5-7).
  • The devastating sequence of verses 9-10: break the commandments in the street, then stand in the house called by His name and announce "we are delivered" - as though the building laundered the conduct.
  • "A den of robbers" is not a charge of commerce but of refuge - a cave where criminals feel safe between jobs. It is the line Yahshua will quote when He clears the courts (Jeremiah 7:11).
  • Go to Shiloh and look. Yahweh had put His name there first, and the ruins were still visible. Election of a place has never been a guarantee against judgment on it (Jeremiah 7:12-15).
  • The second prohibition on intercession: do not pray for this people, for I will not hear you (Jeremiah 7:16).
  • Family idolatry described as a household craft - children gathering wood, fathers kindling, women kneading cakes for the queen of the sky. Everyone has a job (Jeremiah 7:17-20).
  • The sacrificial system is put in its place: what Yahweh commanded at the exodus was not burnt offerings first but "listen to my voice" (Jeremiah 7:21-26).
  • Topheth in the valley of Hinnom - child sacrifice Yahweh says never entered His mind - will be renamed the Valley of Slaughter (Jeremiah 7:30-34).

Jeremiah 7 - Opening Intro

Imagine being posted at the door of your own congregation on a feast day, told to say this to people as they arrive with their offerings. That is the assignment.

And the message is not that the temple is worthless. Yahweh built it. He put His name in it. The message is that they have started treating it the way people treat a lucky object - saying the words three times and assuming the words do the work.

So He sends them on a field trip. Go to Shiloh. That is where the tabernacle stood for centuries, where Hannah prayed and Samuel heard his name in the night. Go stand in what is left of it. Yahweh's name having been in a place is not the same as Yahweh being obligated to that place.

Then verses 5 through 7 tell us what would actually have changed the outcome, and it is striking how little of it is religious in the narrow sense. Deal justly with your neighbor. Do not squeeze the immigrant, the orphan, the widow. Do not shed innocent blood.

Before we read, sit with the hard question this chapter asks: whether the things that make us feel secure before God are things He actually asked of us.

CHAPTER 8 - The Stork Knows Her Seasons, and No Balm in Gilead

Key Notes

  • The bones of kings, priests, and prophets will be exhumed and spread before the sun, moon, and host of heaven they worshipped - the gods they served given their remains (Jeremiah 8:1-3).
  • Two questions expose the unnaturalness of the situation: do men fall and not get up? Does one turn away and not return? Backsliding has become perpetual, a permanent condition rather than an episode (Jeremiah 8:4-5).
  • Nobody says the four words that would change everything: "What have I done?" Instead each rushes on his course like a warhorse charging into battle (Jeremiah 8:6).
  • The stork, turtledove, swallow, and crane all keep their appointed times. Migratory birds are more attentive to Yahweh's calendar than the people who received His Torah (Jeremiah 8:7).
  • A claim to wisdom is met with a charge against the scribes: the pen that should have preserved Torah has worked falsely (Jeremiah 8:8-9).
  • The indictment from chapter 6 returns nearly word for word - covetousness from least to greatest, "peace, peace" where there is none, and a people who have lost the capacity to blush (Jeremiah 8:10-12).
  • "The harvest is past. The summer has ended, and we are not saved." A season that had a limit, and was allowed to close (Jeremiah 8:20).
  • Jeremiah's identification with the wound is total: for the hurt of my people, I am hurt (Jeremiah 8:21).
  • The chapter closes with three questions and no answer. Gilead was famous for balm; physicians existed. The medicine was not lacking - the patient refused it (Jeremiah 8:22).

Jeremiah 8 - Opening Intro

Verse 7 is one of the quietest rebukes in the prophets, and one of the sharpest. A stork does not consult a calendar. It simply knows. So does the turtledove, the swallow, the crane. They keep appointments they were never taught.

And Judah, who was taught - who had the feasts written down, the sabbaths marked, the whole rhythm of Yahweh's year handed to them in writing - does not know.

Then verse 20, which may be the saddest sentence in the book. Harvest past. Summer ended. And we are not saved. It is the voice of people realizing that a window they assumed would stay open had a closing date all along, and that they had been busy during it.

And finally the three questions of verse 22, which are not really questions. Gilead had balm - it was the region's famous export, carried by the caravan that bought Joseph. There were physicians. The supply was never the problem.

Before we read, hold this honestly: the ache in that last verse is not that healing was unavailable. It is that it was right there, and went unused.

CHAPTER 9 - A Fountain of Tears and the Only Boast Worth Having

Key Notes

  • The chapter opens with a wish rather than an oracle: that his head were waters and his eyes a spring of tears, so the weeping could match the loss (Jeremiah 9:1).
  • His second wish is escape - a traveller's lodge in the wilderness, anywhere but among them. Faithfulness and the desire to leave are not mutually exclusive (Jeremiah 9:2).
  • The central sin of this chapter is speech. Tongues bent like bows, aimed and drawn, trained to lie until lying takes effort - "they weary themselves committing iniquity" (Jeremiah 9:3-5).
  • Social trust has collapsed to the point that Yahweh warns them against their own brothers and neighbors. Deceit is the habitation they live inside (Jeremiah 9:4-6).
  • The link between deceit and ignorance of God is made explicit twice: through deceit, they refuse to know me. Lying is not merely a symptom; it is how they keep from knowing Him (Jeremiah 9:3, 6).
  • The tongue is a drawn arrow: peaceable words in the mouth while the heart prepares an ambush (Jeremiah 9:8).
  • Professional mourning women are summoned because the grief has exceeded amateur capacity, and mothers are told to teach their daughters lamentation - a generation raised in grief (Jeremiah 9:17-21).
  • The great reversal of verses 23-24: not wisdom, not might, not riches. The only boast permitted is understanding and knowing Yahweh - and what one knows about Him is that He delights in loving kindness, justice, and righteousness in the earth (Jeremiah 9:23-24).
  • The chapter ends by collapsing the distinction the people relied on most: circumcised in flesh, uncircumcised in heart, listed alongside Egypt, Edom, Ammon, and Moab (Jeremiah 9:25-26).

Jeremiah 9 - Opening Intro

This is where the title "the weeping prophet" comes from, and it is worth noticing that Jeremiah does not present his tears as a strength. He presents them as insufficient. He wants more water than he has.

It is also worth noticing his second wish, in verse 2. He wants out. A lodge in the desert, away from all of them. The man who will not stop delivering the message would very much like to stop living among the people he delivers it to. Faithfulness does not require pretending you enjoy it.

The sin under the microscope here is speech - and the diagnosis is precise. They have taught their tongue to speak lies. Taught. It took practice. And they weary themselves at it, which means deceit has become laborious, a full-time occupation requiring maintenance.

And then, out of the middle of all that grief, comes verses 23 and 24 - one of the great summary statements of scripture. Three things people build their lives on, all disqualified. One thing left: knowing Him. And what you find when you know Him is not raw power but loving kindness, justice, and righteousness - and that He delights in them.

Before we read, carry this: the chapter that weeps hardest is also the chapter that tells us most plainly what God is actually like.

Major Themes Across Jeremiah 7-9

  • A holy place treated as a charm - the address mistaken for the relationship
  • Shiloh as the standing proof that Yahweh's name on a site is no guarantee for the site
  • Worship measured at the gate: the foreigner, the fatherless, and the widow as the actual test
  • Obedience commanded before sacrifice, at the exodus and ever since
  • Migratory birds keeping appointed times that a Torah-holding people no longer recognize
  • A season with a closing date - harvest past, summer ended, and the window shut
  • Balm available and physicians present, with the wound left untreated by choice
  • Speech as the engine of estrangement - tongues trained to lie until deceit becomes hard work
  • Grief so large it requires professional mourners and has to be taught to the next generation
  • Wisdom, might, and riches disqualified as boasts; knowing Yahweh left standing alone
  • Circumcision of the flesh without circumcision of the heart, counted with the nations

Benediction

Let this be the word that goes before us tonight:

May the God who sent His prophet to the gate give us the courage to hear correction in the places where we feel most secure, and may we never mistake the building for the covenant.

May He make our worship legible at the gate - in how we treat the foreigner, the fatherless, and the widow - so that what we sing inside is confirmed by what we do outside.

May He teach us to say the four words this generation would not say: what have I done? And may He meet that question with mercy, as He always has.

May we know the season we are in, as surely as the stork knows hers, and may we not let the harvest pass while we are busy with lesser things.

May He heal what we have left untreated, and may we stop refusing the balm that has been within reach the whole time.

May He tame the tongue no one can tame, and make our speech a place where our neighbors are safe.

And may we boast in nothing but this - that we know Him, who delights in loving kindness, justice, and righteousness in the earth.

May every promise He has spoken over us stand up, come forth, and manifest in its appointed time.

Amen.
