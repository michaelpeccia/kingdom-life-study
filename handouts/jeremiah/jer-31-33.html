Tonight we reach the summit of the book. Everything Jeremiah has carried - the belt, the drought, the yoke, the broken jar - has been clearing ground for these three chapters.

A love called everlasting, and a God who says He drew us before we ever turned.

A covenant that will not be written on stone but on the inside of a person.

A prophet in a prison cell buying real estate in a country that is about to be destroyed.

Jeremiah 31 gathers the scattered, dries the eyes of Rachel, retires an old proverb about sour grapes, and then announces a new covenant that the New Testament will quote at greater length than any other Old Testament passage.

Jeremiah 32 puts the prophet under arrest during the siege and tells him to buy a field - and then lets him admit to Yahweh's face that the command makes no sense.

Jeremiah 33 opens with an invitation that has kept people praying for twenty-six centuries: call to me, and I will answer you, and will show you great and difficult things which you don't know.

This is not just history.

This is a mirror.

And tonight, we let the Word fill us.

CHAPTER 31 - Everlasting Love and the New Covenant

Key Notes

  • The chapter opens with the covenant formula stretched to its widest reach - not Judah only, but "all the families of Israel," including the northern tribes gone for a century (Jeremiah 31:1).
  • "I have loved you with an everlasting love. Therefore I have drawn you with loving kindness." The drawing is the consequence of the loving, not its reward (Jeremiah 31:3).
  • The return is pictured as a party, not a procession - tambourines, dancing, vineyards planted on the mountains of Samaria by people who will live to eat the fruit (Jeremiah 31:4-5).
  • The gathered company is deliberately unimpressive: the blind, the lame, the pregnant, the woman in labor. Nobody is left behind for slowing the column down (Jeremiah 31:8).
  • "He who scattered Israel will gather him." The same hand does both, which is why the exile was never a sign that He had lost track of them (Jeremiah 31:10).
  • Rachel is heard weeping in Ramah - the staging town for deportation - and is told to stop, because "there is hope for your latter end" (Jeremiah 31:15-17).
  • Ephraim's repentance is quoted back with a prayer inside it: "Turn me, and I will be turned." Even the turning is asked for as a gift (Jeremiah 31:18).
  • Yahweh's answer is the most emotional line in the book: "my heart yearns for him. I will surely have mercy on him." The Hebrew is visceral, the language of a parent's insides (Jeremiah 31:20).
  • The four demolition verbs of chapter 1 are answered at last - He watched to pluck up and break down; now He watches to build and to plant (Jeremiah 31:28).
  • A proverb is formally retired. No more "the fathers have eaten sour grapes" - each person will die for his own iniquity, and the excuse of inherited fate goes with it (Jeremiah 31:29-30).
  • Then the new covenant: not like the one they broke, but Torah written on the inward parts, a knowledge of Yahweh needing no intermediary, and sin remembered no more (Jeremiah 31:31-34).
  • The guarantee is cosmological. Israel's continuance is pinned to the fixed order of sun, moon, and sea - if those fail, then and only then does the promise fail (Jeremiah 31:35-37).
  • The chapter ends by taking the valley of dead bodies and ashes - the Hinnom of chapter 19 - and declaring it holy to Yahweh, never to be plucked up again (Jeremiah 31:38-40).

Jeremiah 31 - Opening Intro

Start with verse 3, because everything else in this chapter runs downhill from it. "I have loved you with an everlasting love. Therefore I have drawn you with loving kindness." Read the logic carefully. He did not draw them and then decide to love them. The love came first, and the drawing is what the love does.

That order matters enormously by the time you reach verse 18, where Ephraim prays the strangest and most honest prayer in the book: "Turn me, and I will be turned." He does not say I will turn. He asks to be turned. He has apparently tried, and found that repentance is something he needs done to him before he can do it.

And Yahweh's reply in verse 20 is worth reading aloud. The Hebrew word behind "my heart yearns" refers to the inward parts - the churn in your gut when you see someone you love in trouble. It is the same root behind the word for a mother's womb. This is not a formal pardon issued from a bench. This is a father who cannot help himself.

Then comes verse 31, and the phrase that gives the second half of the Bible its name. A new covenant - berit hadashah, which the Greek renders kaine diatheke. Notice what is new about it and what is not. The content is not replaced: "I will put my law in their inward parts." It is the same Torah. What changes is the location. It moves from tablets to tissue, from an external standard to an internal instinct.

And the last clause is the one that makes it good news rather than merely a better system: "I will forgive their iniquity, and I will remember their sin no more." Hebrews quotes these four verses twice, at greater length than any other Old Testament passage in the New Testament, and both times the writer lands on that line.

Before we read, hold the very last verse next to chapter 19. That valley of dead bodies and ashes is Hinnom - the place where children were burned, the place Yahweh renamed the Valley of Slaughter. Here He says it will be holy to Yahweh, and never plucked up again. If He will do that with that valley, there is nothing in your history He cannot consecrate.

CHAPTER 32 - The Field Bought Under Siege

Key Notes

  • The date is the tenth year of Zedekiah, eighteenth of Nebuchadnezzar - roughly a year before the walls fall. The siege is already underway (Jeremiah 32:1-2).
  • Jeremiah is under arrest in the court of the guard, jailed by his own king for saying the city would be taken - which it is about to be (Jeremiah 32:2-5).
  • Yahweh tells him in advance that his cousin Hanamel will show up wanting to sell a field, and when Hanamel walks in, Jeremiah says simply, "Then I knew that this was Yahweh's word" (Jeremiah 32:6-8).
  • The transaction is recorded in almost comic legal detail: seventeen shekels weighed out, the deed signed and sealed, an open copy and a sealed copy, witnesses called, everything filed (Jeremiah 32:9-12).
  • The deeds go into an earthen jar "that they may last many days" - the same kind of jar in which the Dead Sea Scrolls would survive six centuries in the Judean desert (Jeremiah 32:14).
  • The point is stated in one sentence: "Houses and fields and vineyards will yet again be bought in this land." The purchase is a prophecy performed with money (Jeremiah 32:15).
  • Then, remarkably, Jeremiah prays - and his prayer begins with confident theology and ends in open confusion. "There is nothing too hard for you" is verse 17 (Jeremiah 32:16-23).
  • By verse 25 the confidence has run out: "You have said to me, Buy the field... whereas the city is given into the hand of the Chaldeans." He obeyed first and questioned after (Jeremiah 32:24-25).
  • Yahweh answers by turning Jeremiah's own sentence into a question: "Is there anything too hard for me?" The prophet's theology is handed back to him as an examination (Jeremiah 32:27).
  • The indictment that follows is comprehensive - kings, princes, priests, prophets, and people - and returns to the valley of Hinnom, and to the phrase "it didn't even come into my mind" (Jeremiah 32:28-35).
  • And then "Now therefore" - and the whole direction reverses. Gathered, brought back, dwelling safely, one heart and one way, an everlasting covenant, His fear put inside them (Jeremiah 32:36-40).
  • The most startling promise is verse 41: "I will rejoice over them to do them good... with my whole heart and with my whole soul." God describes Himself as wholehearted about us (Jeremiah 32:41-44).

Jeremiah 32 - Opening Intro

Consider the position Jeremiah is in when this chapter opens. He is in custody. The Babylonian army is camped outside the walls. The land around Anathoth - three miles north of Jerusalem - is almost certainly already occupied. And his cousin walks into the prison yard with a real estate offer.

Every instinct says no. The property is worthless. The seller knows it is worthless, which is precisely why he is selling. And the buyer is a man who has spent forty years announcing that this exact catastrophe was coming, so nobody could accuse Jeremiah of not seeing it clearly.

He buys it anyway. Seventeen shekels, weighed out on a balance, in a besieged city, for a field he will never plow.

And the legal detail in verses 9 through 12 is not padding. Two copies - one sealed for the archive, one open for inspection - witnesses, signatures, the whole apparatus. Jeremiah is not making a gesture. He is creating a document that will still be valid in seventy years, and he puts it in a clay jar so it will survive, which is exactly how the Dead Sea Scrolls came down to us.

Here is what makes this chapter honest rather than merely inspiring: he obeys completely, and then he prays a prayer that falls apart. Verse 17 is magnificent - "there is nothing too hard for you." Verse 25 is a man with his hands open, saying the equivalent of: I did what you said, and I do not understand it. The siege ramps are up. Why am I buying dirt?

And Yahweh's answer is to quote him back to himself. "Is there anything too hard for me?" He does not explain the field. He asks whether Jeremiah believed what he just said.

Before we read, notice where the chapter ends up. Not with the deed, but with verse 41: "I will rejoice over them to do them good... with my whole heart and with my whole soul." The same God who spent thirty chapters on judgment describes His intention toward His people as wholehearted delight. Both are true, and only one of them is the last word.

CHAPTER 33 - Call to Me, and I Will Answer

Key Notes

  • The setting is unchanged - still the court of the guard, still under arrest. Verse 1 says the word came "the second time," which means the encouragement of chapter 32 was not the end of it (Jeremiah 33:1).
  • Yahweh introduces Himself by what He does rather than by what He is: the One who does it, who forms it, who establishes it (Jeremiah 33:2).
  • "Call to me, and I will answer you, and will show you great and difficult things, which you don't know." The invitation is issued to a man in a cell, about a future he cannot see (Jeremiah 33:3).
  • The city's houses are being torn down for rubble to plug the breaches - and it will not work. The promise comes with the demolition still audible (Jeremiah 33:4-5).
  • "I will bring it health and cure" answers the incurable wound of chapter 30 directly, and adds "abundance of peace and truth" (Jeremiah 33:6).
  • Forgiveness is stated twice for emphasis - cleansed from all iniquity, pardoned for all iniquities - and it precedes the rebuilding rather than following it (Jeremiah 33:8).
  • The purpose of the restoration is missional: this city becomes a name of joy before all the nations of the earth, who hear the good and tremble at it (Jeremiah 33:9).
  • The silenced sounds of chapter 7 and chapter 25 come back one by one - joy, gladness, bridegroom, bride, and the thanksgiving refrain sung in Yahweh's house (Jeremiah 33:10-11).
  • The Branch oracle of chapter 23 returns, with one striking change: there the name Yahweh Our Righteousness belonged to Him; here it is given to the city (Jeremiah 33:15-16).
  • Two guarantees are attached - a descendant of David on the throne, and Levites offering continually - and both are tied to the covenant of day and night (Jeremiah 33:17-22).
  • The people are quoted saying the quiet thing out loud: "Has Yahweh cast off the two families which he chose?" And Yahweh answers by pointing at the sunrise (Jeremiah 33:23-26).

Jeremiah 33 - Opening Intro

Verse 3 has been embroidered onto more wall hangings than almost any sentence in Jeremiah, and like 29:11 it is usually lifted clean out of its address. So look at where the man receiving it is standing.

He is locked up. The siege ramps are finished. The houses of Jerusalem are being demolished for rubble to plug the holes in the wall, and verse 5 says plainly that it will not work. That is the room in which God says: call to me, and I will answer you, and will show you great and difficult things which you don't know.

The Hebrew word behind "difficult" carries the sense of fortified, inaccessible - things walled off from you. It is an invitation to ask about what you cannot get to on your own. And it is offered to a prisoner, which should tell us something about where God does some of His best talking.

Then watch this chapter systematically undo the book. Chapter 30 said the wound was incurable; verse 6 says health and cure. Chapters 7 and 25 said the voice of the bridegroom and the bride would cease; verse 11 puts it back, along with the thanksgiving refrain the returning exiles would actually sing when the foundation was relaid. The demolition list is being read backward.

And there is a detail in verse 16 that is easy to miss. In chapter 23 the Branch is given the name Yahweh Tsidkenu - Yahweh Our Righteousness. Here the identical name is given to Jerusalem. What belongs to Him is put on her. If you have ever wondered where the New Testament gets the idea of an imputed righteousness, a name transferred from the King to the city He saves, it is sitting right here in a prophecy delivered from a prison.

Before we read, notice how the chapter answers despair. The people are asking whether God has finally cast off the two families He chose. He does not argue. He points out the window and says: if you can cancel tomorrow morning, then you can cancel this.

Major Themes Across Jeremiah 31-33

  • Love that precedes the drawing - everlasting first, and the turning as its consequence
  • Repentance asked for as a gift: "Turn me, and I will be turned"
  • A God whose insides churn over His people, described in the language of a parent rather than a judge
  • Inherited fate retired - each person answering for his own iniquity, and the sour-grapes excuse withdrawn
  • A new covenant whose newness is location rather than content: the same Torah, moved from stone to heart
  • Sin forgiven and deliberately not remembered, as the ground of the whole arrangement
  • Obedience before understanding - a field bought at full price in a city about to fall
  • Honest prayer that begins in confidence and ends in confusion, and is answered rather than rebuked
  • God's own question handed back to us: is there anything too hard for me?
  • Promises anchored to sunrise and sea, so that the covenant fails only if creation does
  • The demolition list read backward - silenced joy restored, an incurable wound healed, Hinnom made holy

Benediction

Let this be the word that goes before us tonight:

May the God who loved us with an everlasting love before we ever turned toward Him settle that order in us for good, so that we stop trying to earn what has already been given.

May He turn us where we cannot turn ourselves, and may we have the honesty of Ephraim to ask for it rather than pretend we managed it.

May He write His Torah on our inward parts until obedience stops feeling like a foreign language, and may we know Him ourselves and not only by report.

May He forgive our iniquity and remember our sin no more, and may we stop remembering what He has chosen to forget.

May we obey before we understand, buying the field when the siege ramps are up, and may He receive our confused prayers afterward without rebuke.

May we answer His question honestly - is anything too hard for Him - and live as though we believed our own confession.

And may He take the valley of dead bodies and ashes in each of our histories and make it holy to Yahweh, never to be plucked up or thrown down again.

May every promise He has spoken over us stand up, come forth, and manifest in its appointed time.

Amen.
