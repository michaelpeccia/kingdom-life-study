Tonight we begin Jeremiah at the beginning - with a young man told he was known before he was formed, a courtroom where Yahweh puts His own conduct on trial, and a wife the law says cannot come home who is told to come home anyway.

A boy who says he cannot speak, and a hand that touches his mouth.

A people who traded a spring of living water for cisterns they cracked themselves.

A God who writes the divorce certificate and then, against every legal precedent He Himself gave, says "return again to me."

Jeremiah 1 is the call - the commission, the two visions, and the promise that turns a frightened young priest into a fortified city.

Jeremiah 2 is the covenant lawsuit, where Yahweh does something startling: instead of listing charges first, He asks what He did wrong. "What unrighteousness have your fathers found in me?" The indictment only lands after the question has been left hanging.

Jeremiah 3 takes the lawsuit into the most painful room in the house - the marriage - and there, where the case is strongest and the verdict most deserved, mercy interrupts the proceedings.

This is not just history.

This is a mirror.

And tonight, we let the Word fill us.

CHAPTER 1 - The Call, the Almond Branch, and the Boiling Pot

Key Notes

  • The book is dated precisely - the thirteenth year of Josiah through the eleventh year of Zedekiah, to the captivity of Jerusalem. Jeremiah's ministry spans roughly forty years and five kings (Jeremiah 1:1-3).
  • Yahweh's commission reaches backward before Jeremiah existed: known, sanctified, and appointed a prophet to the nations before he was formed in the womb (Jeremiah 1:4-5).
  • Jeremiah objects on the grounds of youth and inability - "I don't know how to speak; for I am a child" - and Yahweh does not argue with the self-assessment. He simply overrides it with a command and a promise (Jeremiah 1:6-8).
  • Yahweh touches his mouth: the words will not be Jeremiah's own. The authority of the message rests on its source, not on the speaker's eloquence (Jeremiah 1:9).
  • The commission is sixfold and deliberately lopsided - four verbs of demolition, two of construction: to uproot, tear down, destroy, overthrow, build, and plant (Jeremiah 1:10).
  • First vision, the almond branch: a Hebrew wordplay that does not survive translation. The almond (shaqed) is the watcher (shoqed) - the first tree to wake in late winter. Yahweh is watching over His word to perform it (Jeremiah 1:11-12).
  • Second vision, the boiling cauldron tipping from the north: the direction from which every invader historically came down into Judah, and the direction of the coming Babylonian judgment (Jeremiah 1:13-16).
  • The chapter ends with the promise that carries the whole book: a fortified city, an iron pillar, bronze walls. Not exemption from the fight - guaranteed survival of it (Jeremiah 1:17-19).

Jeremiah 1 - Opening Intro

Everything Jeremiah will endure over the next forty years is answered in advance, right here, before he has preached a single sermon. That is worth noticing. Yahweh does not call him and then explain the cost later; He tells him at the outset that the whole land will fight him, and in the same sentence tells him how it ends.

Notice also what Yahweh does with Jeremiah's objection. "I am a child" is almost certainly true - most scholars place him in his late teens or early twenties. Yahweh never says it is false. He says it is irrelevant. The qualification was never the speaker; it was the sending.

And then two visions that set the shape of the book. An almond branch, because Yahweh is awake and watching over what He said. A pot boiling over from the north, because what He said is coming.

Before we read, hold this: a calling that reaches back before your birth is not a calling you talked yourself into, and therefore not one you can talk yourself out of.

CHAPTER 2 - The Covenant Lawsuit and the Broken Cisterns

Key Notes

  • Yahweh opens not with accusation but with memory - and with tenderness. He remembers the kindness of Israel's youth and the love of her wedding days, when she followed Him into an unsown wilderness (Jeremiah 2:1-3).
  • Then the question that frames the entire lawsuit: "What unrighteousness have your fathers found in me?" The burden of proof is placed on the accused, and the silence that follows is the verdict (Jeremiah 2:4-5).
  • Nobody asked "Where is Yahweh?" - not the people who forgot the wilderness deliverance, and not the priests whose whole office was to know Him. The failure runs top to bottom (Jeremiah 2:6-8).
  • Yahweh calls the heavens as witnesses to something no pagan nation has ever done: exchange its gods. Israel swapped her glory for what cannot profit (Jeremiah 2:9-13).
  • The two evils, stated with surgical precision: forsaking the spring of living waters, and hewing out broken cisterns that hold no water. One sin of abandonment, one of substitution (Jeremiah 2:13).
  • The noble vine, planted as pure seed, has become the degenerate branches of a foreign vine - the corruption is not in the planting but in the turning (Jeremiah 2:20-21).
  • The stain will not scrub out. Lye and much soap cannot reach what Yahweh sees, because the problem is not on the surface (Jeremiah 2:22).
  • The chapter closes on the most dangerous sentence in it: "I have not sinned." Judgment comes not merely for the sin, but for the denial of it (Jeremiah 2:23-37).

Jeremiah 2 - Opening Intro

This chapter is built like a courtroom transcript, and Yahweh argues His own case. But watch how He opens it. Not with the charge sheet - with a wedding photograph. "I remember for you the kindness of your youth, the love of your weddings." Before He says one word about what they have done, He says what they were.

Then comes the question that no one in the room can answer: what did I do wrong? Every accusation in the rest of the chapter rests on that unanswered question. They did not leave because He failed them. They left because they wanted to.

And at the center sits the image the whole book keeps returning to. Two evils - not one. They walked away from a spring that never stops running, and then did the exhausting work of chiseling storage tanks out of rock, and the tanks leaked. Nobody digs a cistern next to a living spring unless something has gone wrong in the wanting.

Before we read, carry this honestly: the labor we pour into our broken cisterns is usually the clearest measure of how far we have walked from the spring.

CHAPTER 3 - The Divorce Certificate and the Word "Return"

Key Notes

  • The chapter opens on a legal impossibility. Torah forbids a man from taking back a wife who has married another - and Yahweh cites the very statute against Himself before saying "yet return again to me" (Jeremiah 3:1).
  • The withheld rain is named as covenant discipline, but it produced no shame: "you have had a prostitute's forehead and you refused to be ashamed" (Jeremiah 3:2-5).
  • Two sisters are compared. Backsliding Israel, the northern kingdom, was divorced and exiled; treacherous Judah watched it happen and did the same thing anyway (Jeremiah 3:6-10).
  • The verdict is genuinely shocking: "Backsliding Israel has shown herself more righteous than treacherous Judah." Open apostasy is judged less severely than pretended repentance (Jeremiah 3:10-11).
  • The message is aimed north, toward a kingdom already gone into exile - people with no temple, no king, and no standing. They are the first to hear "Return" (Jeremiah 3:12).
  • The only condition attached is acknowledgment: "Only acknowledge your iniquity." Not restitution first, not reformation first - naming it (Jeremiah 3:13).
  • The promise of shepherds after Yahweh's own heart, feeding with knowledge and understanding - a direct answer to the failed shepherds of chapter 2 (Jeremiah 3:15).
  • A day is coming when the ark itself will not be missed, because Jerusalem will be called Yahweh's Throne and the nations will be gathered to it - the symbol retired because the reality has arrived (Jeremiah 3:16-18).
  • The chapter ends with the response Yahweh has been waiting for since chapter 2 - a confession that finally names the sin instead of denying it (Jeremiah 3:21-25).

Jeremiah 3 - Opening Intro

Chapter 3 begins with Yahweh quoting His own Torah against Himself. Deuteronomy 24 is explicit: a woman who leaves her husband and marries another cannot be taken back. The land would be polluted. It is an abomination.

Then, having established that He has every legal right to be done, He says: "yet return again to me."

This is the tension the whole chapter lives in, and it is not resolved by softening the law. The law stands. Something else has to give - and what gives is Yahweh Himself, who will eventually satisfy His own statute rather than ignore it.

Notice too who hears the invitation first. Not Judah, who still has the temple and the throne and the appearance of faithfulness. The word goes north, to the kingdom already carried off, already divorced, already written off. The ones with nothing left to bargain with are the ones told to come home.

And notice what is asked of them. Not a program. Not a probation period. "Only acknowledge your iniquity." After two chapters of "I have not sinned," the door opens the moment someone tells the truth.

Major Themes Across Jeremiah 1-3

  • A calling that predates the called - known, sanctified, and appointed before being formed
  • The qualification for speaking is never eloquence but sending; the mouth is touched, not trained
  • Yahweh watching over His word to perform it - the almond branch as the guarantee of the boiling pot
  • A lawsuit that opens with a wedding memory and a question: what unrighteousness did you find in me?
  • Two evils, not one - forsaking the spring, and the exhausting labor of digging cisterns that leak
  • Denial as the deeper guilt: "I have not sinned" is what finally brings the verdict
  • Mercy that cites the law it is about to overrule, and calls home a wife with no legal standing to return
  • Acknowledgment as the only condition - the door opens the moment the truth is told
  • The symbol retired because the reality arrives - an ark not missed, a throne in its place

Benediction

Let this be the word that goes before us tonight:

May the God who knew us before He formed us settle the question of whether we belong to Him, and may we stop auditioning for a place that was decided before we drew breath.

May He touch our mouths, so that what we carry to others is His word and not our opinion, and may He make us unafraid of faces.

May He keep watch over every word He has spoken to us, as the almond keeps watch over the end of winter, and may we learn to wait for its budding without doubting the tree.

May He show us the cisterns we have dug, and give us the honesty to stop repairing them, and may He lead us back to the spring we left.

May He deliver us from the words "I have not sinned," and give us instead the courage of a plain acknowledgment, knowing it is the one thing He asks before He heals.

May He be to us the Husband who reads the law aloud and then calls us home anyway, and may we not mistake His patience for indifference or His mercy for permission.

And may every promise He has spoken over us stand up, come forth, and manifest in its appointed time.

Amen.
