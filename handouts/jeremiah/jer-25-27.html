Tonight the book widens. For twenty-four chapters Jeremiah has been speaking to Judah. Here he is handed a cup and told to carry it to the nations.

Twenty-three years of preaching, summarized in one sentence: but you have not listened.

Seventy years put on a calendar - the first time the exile is given an end date.

A prophet walking through Jerusalem with a wooden yoke strapped to his own neck.

A trial in the temple court where the charge is a sermon and the penalty is death.

Jeremiah 25 turns a local judgment into a global one, and puts the cup of wrath in the hand of every kingdom on the earth - beginning, uncomfortably, with the city that bears Yahweh's name.

Jeremiah 26 puts the prophet on trial for the temple sermon, and is saved by the memory of an old prophecy and the courage of one official who refused to hand him over.

Jeremiah 27 asks Judah to do the humiliating thing: put your neck under the yoke and live - and warns that the voices promising a quick end to it were never sent.

This is not just history.

This is a mirror.

And tonight, we let the Word fill us.

CHAPTER 25 - Twenty-Three Years, Seventy Years, and the Cup No Nation Refuses

Key Notes

  • The chapter is dated to Jehoiakim's fourth year - the same year Nebuchadnezzar took the throne. Two clocks start together, and one of them is running out (Jeremiah 25:1-2).
  • Jeremiah counts his own ministry: twenty-three years of speaking, "rising up early and speaking; but you have not listened." The idiom pictures a man who gets up before dawn to try again (Jeremiah 25:3).
  • He was not alone. Yahweh sent "all his servants the prophets," and the response to every one of them was the same silence (Jeremiah 25:4).
  • The message they all carried was startlingly modest: turn from evil, don't chase other gods, and you may stay in the land. The offer was never complicated (Jeremiah 25:5-6).
  • Nebuchadnezzar is called "my servant" - a pagan emperor given the same title as Moses and David, because sovereignty is not limited to those who acknowledge it (Jeremiah 25:9).
  • The judgment is described by what will go missing: mirth, gladness, bridegroom, bride, the sound of millstones, the light of a lamp. Ordinary domestic noise, and then nothing (Jeremiah 25:10).
  • Then comes the number that changes everything. Seventy years - the first time this catastrophe is given a boundary. Exile is announced with an expiry date attached (Jeremiah 25:11).
  • And Babylon is not exempt. When the seventy years are done, the instrument of judgment is judged in turn, for exactly the same reason: iniquity (Jeremiah 25:12-14).
  • Jeremiah is handed the cup of the wine of wrath and told to make the nations drink. The vision moves from Jerusalem outward through Egypt, Philistia, Edom, Moab, Ammon, Tyre, Arabia, Elam, Media, and finally Sheshach - a cipher for Babylon itself (Jeremiah 25:15-26).
  • The most sobering line in the chapter is the order of service: "I begin to work evil at the city which is called by my name." Judgment starts at the household of God (Jeremiah 25:29).
  • The imagery turns cosmic - Yahweh roaring from His holy habitation, treading grapes, entering into judgment with all flesh, a great storm rising from the ends of the earth (Jeremiah 25:30-33).
  • The chapter closes on the shepherds again: wail, wallow in dust, no way to flee, no escape - the leaders who lost the flock are the last ones offered cover (Jeremiah 25:34-38).

Jeremiah 25 - Opening Intro

Twenty-three years. That is what verse 3 puts on the table before anything else happens - not a doctrine, an accounting. Jeremiah has been preaching since the thirteenth year of Josiah, through a reformation and a relapse and three kings, and the summary of it fits in five words: you have not listened.

The Hebrew behind "rising up early and speaking" is worth slowing down on. It is a picture of someone who gets up before dawn, day after day, hoping this is the morning it lands. Yahweh uses it of Himself twice in this chapter. That is not the language of a God who lost patience quickly.

Then verse 11 does something the book has not done before. It puts a number on the disaster. Seventy years. Not forever - seventy. Chronicles later reads that number as the land finally collecting the Sabbath rests it had been denied, and Daniel is found reading this very verse in exile and beginning to pray because he realized the clock was nearly done. A boundary is a mercy. It means the thing has an end that God Himself set.

And then the cup. Jeremiah is handed it and told to make the nations drink, and the list runs outward in widening rings from Jerusalem - Egypt, Philistia, Edom, Moab, Tyre, Arabia, Elam, Media - and ends with Sheshach, an old cipher in which the letters of Babel are reversed. Even the hand holding the rod drinks last.

But notice where the pouring starts. Verse 29: "I begin to work evil at the city which is called by my name." Not the pagans first, and the covenant people if there is any wrath left over. His own house, first. Peter says the same thing seven centuries later in almost the same words.

Before we read, hold both halves of this chapter together. The God who sets a seventy-year limit on His people's discipline is the same God who hands the cup to every kingdom on earth. Neither the mercy nor the severity is a mood. Both are exact.

CHAPTER 26 - The Temple Sermon on Trial

Key Notes

  • This is the same sermon as chapter 7, told from the outside: chapter 7 gives the content, chapter 26 gives the consequences (Jeremiah 26:1-2).
  • The commissioning includes a warning that preachers have quoted ever since: "Don't omit a word." The temptation to soften was anticipated (Jeremiah 26:2).
  • The purpose clause is pure mercy: "It may be they will listen... that I may relent." The sermon is delivered in hope of not having to be fulfilled (Jeremiah 26:3).
  • The threat that detonates the chapter is one word: Shiloh. Yahweh had let His own sanctuary there be destroyed once already, and everyone in the courtyard knew it (Jeremiah 26:6).
  • The reaction is immediate and unanimous - priests, prophets, and all the people seize him: "You shall surely die!" The clergy lead the mob (Jeremiah 26:7-9).
  • The civil authorities arrive and convene a court in the entry of the New Gate. Jeremiah's life is spared, in part, by the separation of powers (Jeremiah 26:10-11).
  • His defense is not a legal maneuver. He repeats the sermon, renews the offer of repentance, and then says, "as for me, behold, I am in your hand" (Jeremiah 26:12-14).
  • The one warning he adds is about them, not him: kill me and you bring innocent blood on yourselves and on this city (Jeremiah 26:15).
  • The verdict turns on a precedent from a hundred years earlier: Micah said Zion would be plowed as a field, and Hezekiah repented instead of executing him (Jeremiah 26:16-19).
  • Then the chapter tells a second story with the opposite ending. Uriah preached the same message, fled to Egypt in fear, was extradited, and was killed by Jehoiakim (Jeremiah 26:20-23).
  • Jeremiah lives because one man stood in the gap - "the hand of Ahikam the son of Shaphan was with Jeremiah." Providence often has a name and an address (Jeremiah 26:24).

Jeremiah 26 - Opening Intro

Chapter 7 gave us the sermon. Chapter 26 gives us what it cost.

And the instruction beforehand is the part that should make anyone who has ever had to say a hard thing pause: "Don't omit a word." Yahweh knows exactly which sentence Jeremiah will be tempted to leave out - the one about Shiloh - and forbids the omission in advance.

Shiloh was the pressure point. It was where the tabernacle stood for centuries, and it was destroyed, and archaeology has turned up the burn layer. Everyone in that courtyard knew the story and had quietly decided it could not happen twice. Jeremiah says the quiet part out loud, and the priests move to kill him inside the hour.

Then watch the trial, because two things save him and neither of them is a miracle. The first is a handful of old men who remember their history: Micah said this same thing about Zion a century ago, and Hezekiah's response was repentance rather than execution. Scripture citing Scripture, in a courtroom, saves a man's life.

The second is Ahikam. He gets one verse and no speech. He was Shaphan's son, from the family that found the scroll in Josiah's day, and his hand was with Jeremiah so the crowd did not get him. His son Gedaliah will protect Jeremiah again after the city falls.

But the chapter refuses to be tidy about it. Uriah preached the same message and was hunted down in Egypt and killed. Same word, same courage, different ending. Faithfulness is not a guarantee of survival, and the book will not pretend otherwise.

Before we read, sit with what Jeremiah actually says when his life is in the balance. Not a defense. Not a retraction. "I am in your hand. Do with me what is good and right in your eyes." And then one more warning - for them.

CHAPTER 27 - The Yoke on the Prophet's Own Neck

Key Notes

  • Jeremiah is told to make a yoke - bonds and bars, the working harness of an ox - and wear it himself. He does not describe submission; he carries it (Jeremiah 27:2).
  • The audience is international. Envoys from Edom, Moab, Ammon, Tyre and Sidon are in Jerusalem plotting a coalition, and Jeremiah is to send each of them home with a message (Jeremiah 27:3).
  • The theological ground is laid first: "I have made the earth... I give it to whom it seems right to me." Sovereignty over the nations is grounded in creation, not in covenant (Jeremiah 27:5).
  • Nebuchadnezzar is again called "my servant," and the grant is explicitly temporary - through his son and his son's son, and then Babylon's own turn comes (Jeremiah 27:6-7).
  • The terms are blunt: the nation that puts its neck under the yoke stays on its land and farms it. The nation that refuses is consumed by sword, famine, and pestilence (Jeremiah 27:8-11).
  • The list of voices to ignore is long and modern: prophets, diviners, dreamers, soothsayers, sorcerers - a whole industry unified by telling people what they wanted (Jeremiah 27:9-10).
  • To Zedekiah personally the message is reduced to five words of ordinary imperative: bring your neck under, and live (Jeremiah 27:12).
  • To the priests the issue is the temple vessels. The comfortable prophecy circulating was that the articles taken in 597 would be back shortly, and it was a lie (Jeremiah 27:16).
  • Jeremiah proposes a test that costs nothing and exposes everything: if they are really prophets, let them intercede - and see whether the rest of the vessels stay (Jeremiah 27:18).
  • The chapter ends where every hard word in this book eventually ends. The vessels go to Babylon - and then, "until the day that I visit them," they come home (Jeremiah 27:22).

Jeremiah 27 - Opening Intro

There is a particular humiliation in this chapter that is easy to read past. Jeremiah does not preach about submission. He builds a yoke - the wooden bars and leather straps you put on an ox - and wears it, in public, in a capital city full of foreign diplomats, for as long as it takes.

And what he tells them is politically indefensible. There is a coalition forming in Jerusalem, five nations plus Judah, and it is exactly the kind of alliance any reasonable government would pursue. Jeremiah walks into it wearing a yoke and says: go home and submit.

Notice how he grounds it. Not "because Judah has sinned" - these are foreign kings, and that argument has no purchase on them. He goes back further, to creation: I made the earth and everything on it by my great power, and I give it to whom it seems right to me. The claim is not covenantal. It is proprietary.

Then he gives Babylon an expiry date in the same breath he gives it an empire. Nebuchadnezzar, his son, his son's son - and then many nations and great kings will make him their bondservant. Three generations, and the historical record lands almost exactly there. Submission is commanded, but it is never presented as permanent.

The sharpest moment is verse 18, and it is worth remembering the next time anyone claims to speak for God. Jeremiah proposes a test: if these men really are prophets, let them pray. Not preach - pray. Let them stand between Yahweh and the remaining temple vessels and ask that the articles stay. He is measuring prophetic office by intercession rather than by prediction, and the false prophets have nothing to offer, because men who tell people what they want have never learned to stand in the gap.

Before we read, notice how the chapter ends. Not with the yoke. With the vessels coming back - "until the day that I visit them; then I will bring them up, and restore them to this place." Even a chapter about wearing a harness closes with a homecoming.

Major Themes Across Jeremiah 25-27

  • A God who rises up early to send prophets, and twenty-three years of preaching met with silence
  • Judgment given a boundary - seventy years, an end date set by the One who set the discipline
  • Sovereignty grounded in creation: the earth given to whom He chooses, including to kings who never asked
  • The cup of wrath poured out on every nation, beginning at the city that carries His name
  • The word delivered whole - "don't omit a word" - and the temptation to soften named in advance
  • Shiloh as the standing proof that a sanctuary is not a guarantee
  • Faithfulness without a guarantee of survival: Jeremiah spared, Uriah extradited and killed
  • Providence with a name and an address - one official whose hand kept a prophet alive
  • Submission commanded as the way to live, and never presented as permanent
  • Prophetic office measured by intercession rather than prediction, and the homecoming that ends every hard word

Benediction

Let this be the word that goes before us tonight:

May the God who rose up early to speak to a people who would not listen find us awake, and may we never need twenty-three years to hear what He said the first morning.

May we trust that every discipline He permits has a boundary He set, and may we learn to count the years the way Daniel did - not with despair, but with prayer.

May we hold His sovereignty honestly: that He gives the earth to whom He wills, and that no power over us was ever handed down from anywhere but above.

May He make us people who do not omit the word, and may we be more afraid of softening the truth than of the room's reaction to it.

May we never mistake a building, a heritage, or a history for a guarantee, remembering Shiloh.

May He raise up an Ahikam for every faithful voice under threat, and may He make some of us that person for someone else.

And when His hand puts a yoke on our neck, may we bring the neck under it and live, trusting the One who breaks every yoke in His own appointed time - and who brings the vessels home.

May every promise He has spoken over us stand up, come forth, and manifest in its appointed time.

Amen.
