Tonight we continue through Jeremiah - from a prophet forbidden to marry, mourn, or feast, to sin engraved with an iron pen, to a potter's wheel where a ruined vessel is worked again.

A man whose empty house becomes the sermon.

A heart so deceitful that only its Maker can read it - and a Maker who says He does.

A lump of clay that is marred in the potter's hand and is not thrown away.

Jeremiah 16 strips the prophet of ordinary life so that his loneliness itself preaches the coming silence of Judah - and then, in the same breath, promises a second exodus greater than the first.

Jeremiah 17 sets two men side by side: one a shrub in a salt land, the other a tree by the water - and traces the difference all the way down to where a person's trust actually rests, closing at the city gates with the Sabbath as the test case.

Jeremiah 18 takes us down to the potter's house, where Yahweh's sovereignty is shown to be not a fixed script but a working hand - and where the people answer the offer of mercy with "It is in vain," and begin plotting against the man who carried it.

This is not just history.

This is a mirror.

And tonight, we let the Word fill us.

CHAPTER 16 - A Life Emptied Out as a Sign

Key Notes

  • Jeremiah is forbidden to marry or father children in that place - not as asceticism, but as a living sign: a generation is coming whose sons and daughters will die unlamented and unburied (Jeremiah 16:1-4).
  • He is forbidden to enter the house of mourning, because Yahweh has withdrawn His shalom from this people - and with it His loving kindness and tender mercies (Jeremiah 16:5-7).
  • He is equally forbidden the house of feasting: the voice of mirth and gladness, of bridegroom and bride, will cease before their own eyes and in their own days (Jeremiah 16:8-9).
  • When the people ask "What is our iniquity?" the answer is not vague - their fathers forsook Yahweh and walked after other gods, and this generation has done worse than their fathers (Jeremiah 16:10-13).
  • In the middle of the sentence comes an astonishing promise: a day when Israel's defining testimony will no longer be the exodus from Egypt but a greater gathering out of the land of the north (Jeremiah 16:14-15).
  • Fishermen and hunters will be sent - a total, inescapable search - because Yahweh's eyes are on all their ways and their iniquity is not concealed from Him (Jeremiah 16:16-18).
  • The chapter ends with the nations themselves coming from the ends of the earth, confessing that their fathers inherited nothing but lies, and learning that His name is Yahweh (Jeremiah 16:19-21).

Jeremiah 16 - Opening Intro

Tonight Yahweh does something He has not yet done to Jeremiah. In chapter 13 He took a belt. In chapter 15 He took the prophet's peace. Here He takes his future - no wife, no children, no funerals, no weddings. The man himself becomes the object lesson, and the lesson is loneliness.

But notice where the chapter goes. In the middle of a sentence about exile, Yahweh interrupts Himself with a promise so large it will one day retire Israel's oldest testimony: the day is coming when people will no longer say "as Yahweh lives, who brought us out of Egypt," because something greater will have happened.

Before we read, hold this: the same God who empties a house can fill a nation. Judgment here is never the last word - it is the ground being cleared for a homecoming.

CHAPTER 17 - The Engraved Heart, the Two Trees, and the Sabbath Gate

Key Notes

  • Judah's sin is written with an iron pen and a diamond point - engraved on the tablet of their heart and on the horns of their altars. Their worship itself carries the record of their guilt (Jeremiah 17:1-4).
  • Two men are set side by side. Cursed is the man who trusts in man and relies on strength of flesh: he is a shrub in the desert who will not even recognize good when it arrives (Jeremiah 17:5-6).
  • Blessed is the man whose confidence is in Yahweh: a tree planted by the waters, roots spread to the river, green in the heat and fruitful in the year of drought (Jeremiah 17:7-8).
  • "The heart is deceitful above all things" is not despair but diagnosis - and it is immediately answered by the One who says, "I, Yahweh, search the mind. I try the heart" (Jeremiah 17:9-10).
  • Riches gotten wrongly are like a partridge sitting on eggs she did not lay - they leave in the middle of life, and the end is folly (Jeremiah 17:11).
  • Those who forsake Yahweh, the spring of living waters, are written in the earth - a name in dust, erased by the next wind (Jeremiah 17:12-13).
  • Jeremiah prays the prayer that puts everything in the right hands: "Heal me, O Yahweh, and I will be healed. Save me, and I will be saved" (Jeremiah 17:14-18).
  • The chapter closes at the city gates with a concrete test of covenant loyalty: bear no burden on the Sabbath. Obedience means an enduring city; refusal means an unquenched fire in its gates (Jeremiah 17:19-27).

Jeremiah 17 - Opening Intro

This chapter is where Jeremiah stops describing a nation and starts describing a person - any person, including the one reading it. Sin engraved on a heart. Trust placed somewhere. A root system either reaching for a river or clutching at salt.

And in the middle of it sits the verse most of us have heard quoted and few of us have heard finished. "The heart is deceitful above all things... who can know it?" is a question, not a verdict - and Yahweh answers it in the very next breath: I do. I search it. I try it.

Then, remarkably, the chapter does not end in introspection. It ends at a gate, with a wagon, on a Sabbath. The heart Yahweh searches is proven not in feeling but in whether you unload the cart.

Before we read, carry this honestly: the test of where your trust rests is rarely dramatic. It usually looks like an ordinary decision on an ordinary seventh day.

CHAPTER 18 - The Potter's House, the Refusal, and the Plot

Key Notes

  • Jeremiah is sent down to the potter's house to receive a word he could not have received in the temple - the sermon is the workshop itself (Jeremiah 18:1-3).
  • The vessel is marred in the potter's hand, and the potter does not discard the clay. He makes it again, another vessel, as it seemed good to him to make it (Jeremiah 18:4).
  • The principle is then stated plainly: Yahweh's sovereignty over nations is real and it is responsive. Declared judgment is withdrawn when a nation turns; declared blessing is withdrawn when it rebels (Jeremiah 18:5-10).
  • The offer is issued in the plainest possible terms - "return now, everyone from his evil way" - and is met with the most chilling answer in the book: "It is in vain" (Jeremiah 18:11-12).
  • Yahweh points to the natural world to expose the unnatural thing: the snow of Lebanon does not fail, the cold mountain waters do not dry up, but His people have forgotten Him (Jeremiah 18:13-15).
  • They have been made to stumble out of the ancient paths into byways - roads that were never built up, leading to a land of astonishment and hissing (Jeremiah 18:15-17).
  • The response to the message is a conspiracy against the messenger: priest, wise man, and prophet agree that Jeremiah can simply be struck with the tongue and ignored (Jeremiah 18:18).
  • Jeremiah answers with raw, unedited lament - evil repaid for good, a pit dug for the very soul that stood before Yahweh to speak good on their behalf (Jeremiah 18:19-23).

Jeremiah 18 - Opening Intro

Yahweh does not explain this one. He sends Jeremiah on an errand - down the hill, to a workshop, to watch a man work - and lets the sight do the teaching.

What Jeremiah sees is not a potter smashing a failed pot. He sees a potter whose hands never leave the clay. The vessel is marred, and the wheel keeps turning, and the same hands begin again. That is the picture Yahweh chooses for Himself before He says a single word about judgment.

And it matters enormously that the very next thing He says is conditional. "If that nation turns from their evil, I will relent." The clay is not passive in this passage. It answers. And Judah's answer - "It is in vain" - is what turns a workshop into a courtroom.

Before we read, sit with the tension: the hand that can remake you is the same hand you can refuse. Sovereignty here is not fate. It is a craftsman waiting to see whether the clay will yield.

Major Themes Across Jeremiah 16-18

  • A prophet's own life conscripted as the sermon - obedience that costs marriage, mourning, and company
  • Judgment announced and restoration promised in the same breath, with a second exodus greater than the first
  • Two root systems, two futures - trust in flesh that withers, trust in Yahweh that stays green in drought
  • A heart no one can read except the One who made it, and who says plainly that He searches it
  • The Sabbath as the ordinary, concrete gate where covenant loyalty is either carried or set down
  • Sovereignty as a working hand rather than a fixed script - the marred vessel remade, not discarded
  • The offer of return standing open until a people themselves close it with "It is in vain"
  • The cost of carrying the word: conspiracy from priest, sage, and prophet, and lament brought honestly to Yahweh

Benediction

Let this be the word that goes before us tonight:

May the God who asked Jeremiah to give up his own household for the sake of the message give us hearts willing to be spent, and may He never let our obedience become a performance we admire rather than a life we live.

May He gather us as He promised to gather Israel out of the north country, and may our testimony be not only what He brought us out of, but what He is bringing us into.

May He plant us by the waters, with roots that reach past the dry season, so that when the heat comes we do not notice it the way the desert shrub does - and may our leaf stay green when everything around us browns.

May He search the heart we cannot read ourselves, and may we welcome the searching rather than hide from it, knowing that the One who tries the heart is the One who heals it.

May He teach us to honor His Sabbath as a delight and not a burden, and may our ordinary obedience at ordinary gates testify louder than anything we say.

And when we find ourselves marred on the wheel, may we not leap out of His hands - may we yield, and let the Potter make us again, another vessel, as it seems good to Him to make it.

May every promise He has spoken over us stand up, come forth, and manifest in its appointed time.

Amen.
