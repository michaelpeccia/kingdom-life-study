CHAPTER 43 - Down to Egypt, and a Throne in the Clay

Key Notes

  • Verse 1 is careful to say Jeremiah delivered everything - "all the words... even all these words" - so what follows is a rejection of the complete message, not a misunderstanding of part of it (Jeremiah 43:1).
  • The reply is immediate and personal: "You speak falsely. Yahweh our God has not sent you." They do not argue with the content; they deny the commission (Jeremiah 43:2).
  • The speakers are called "the proud men" - the narrator's one editorial word in the whole scene, and it explains everything else in it (Jeremiah 43:2).
  • Baruch is accused of manipulating Jeremiah. It is easier to attack the secretary than the oracle, and a scribe makes a safer target than a prophet (Jeremiah 43:3).
  • Their charge inverts the actual message. Jeremiah said staying would save them; they claim he is trying to get them killed (Jeremiah 43:3).
  • The disobedience is comprehensive - captains, Johanan, and all the people, from the ones who swore the oath in chapter 42 to the ones who only followed (Jeremiah 43:4).
  • Verse 5 is the cruelest sentence in the chapter: the people carried out of Judah are the very ones who had just come home to it from Moab, Ammon, and Edom (Jeremiah 43:5).
  • The passenger list includes the king's daughters, and Jeremiah and Baruch - the prophet is taken into Egypt as cargo, against the word he had just preached (Jeremiah 43:6).
  • Tahpanhes is a frontier garrison in the eastern Delta, the first Egyptian town on the road from Judah - they stop at the border and settle (Jeremiah 43:7).
  • The word of Yahweh comes to Jeremiah in Egypt. Leaving the land did not put them outside His jurisdiction or His speech (Jeremiah 43:8).
  • The sign-act is public and physical: great stones worked into the mortar of the brick pavement at the entry of Pharaoh's house, in front of witnesses (Jeremiah 43:9).
  • Nebuchadnezzar is called "my servant" for the third time in this book - the emperor is an instrument, not an author (Jeremiah 43:10).
  • Egypt is put on "as a shepherd puts on his garment" - a picture of complete ease, and of a man delousing a cloak before he wears it (Jeremiah 43:12).
  • Beth Shemesh here is Heliopolis, the city of the sun; its obelisks are the pillars that get broken (Jeremiah 43:13).

Jeremiah 43 - Opening Intro

Ten days of waiting, an oath sworn before God, and an answer so tender it made the God of Israel sound like He regretted the whole discipline - and this is the reply. "You speak falsely."

Watch what they do with the accusation, because it is one of the oldest moves there is. They cannot dispute the message, so they attack the commission: Yahweh did not send you. And when that is not quite enough, they go after Baruch. The scribe did this. Somebody got to the prophet. There is always a Baruch when people want to disobey a word without calling it the word of God - a handler, an agenda, an influence that explains the message away.

And notice the shape of the charge in verse 3: he wants to hand us over to the Chaldeans to be killed. That is the precise reverse of what Jeremiah said. He said stay and live. They heard stay and die. Fear does not merely resist a word; it rewrites it, and then argues with the version it wrote.

Then verse 5, which is the sentence to sit with. The people Johanan takes into Egypt are described as "all the remnant of Judah, who had returned from all the nations where they had been driven, to live in the land of Judah." These are the ones who came home. They heard there was a governor and a harvest, and they walked back from Moab and Ammon and Edom to be in the land again. Now they are marched out of it by their own leadership. The return is undone by the people who survived to enjoy it.

Jeremiah goes with them. He does not go willingly and he does not go silently, but he goes - an old man who has been right about everything for forty years and has never once been obeyed, taken across a border he preached against. And the first thing that happens on the other side is that God speaks to him again. That is the mercy hidden in this chapter: disobedience changed the address, not the relationship.

So he takes stones and works them into the wet mortar of the brick pavement at Pharaoh's door, where everyone can watch him do it, and says: a throne is coming here. You ran to the safest square in the strongest empire on earth, and the man you were running from will pitch his canopy directly over the spot where you are standing. Egypt will be picked up and worn like a shepherd shrugging on his coat.

Before we read, hold this next to your own fears. They did not choose Egypt because they loved Egypt. They chose it because it looked safe. The word of God is not a threat to the place we run to - it is the only accurate map of it.

CHAPTER 44 - The Queen of the Sky, and Whose Word Will Stand

Key Notes

  • The Jews are now at Migdol, Tahpanhes, Memphis, and Pathros - the whole length of Egypt from the Delta to the south. A temporary refuge has become a diaspora (Jeremiah 44:1).
  • God argues from evidence they can see with their own eyes: look at Jerusalem, look at the cities of Judah, they are a desolation today (Jeremiah 44:2).
  • The cause is named without hedging - incense burned to other gods, by people who did not even know the gods they were serving (Jeremiah 44:3).
  • "Rising up early and sending them" is the recurring picture of Yahweh in this book: not a distant judge but someone who gets up early to plead (Jeremiah 44:4).
  • Verse 7 reframes idolatry entirely - it is "great evil against your own souls." The first casualty of the sin is the sinner (Jeremiah 44:7).
  • The catalogue in verse 9 is unsparing and even-handed: the fathers, the kings, the kings' wives, your own, and your wives (Jeremiah 44:9).
  • "They are not humbled even to this day" - after the siege, the famine, the burning of the temple, and the deportation, nothing has landed (Jeremiah 44:10).
  • Even the harshest oracle keeps a clause open: none will return "except those who will escape," and verse 28 confirms a few will (Jeremiah 44:14).
  • Verse 15 begins the only recorded instance in Scripture of a whole congregation answering a prophet out loud - and the answer is "we will not listen to you" (Jeremiah 44:15-16).
  • The queen of the sky is the Ishtar-Astarte cult, served with poured drink offerings and cakes pressed into her image - domestic worship, run out of the kitchen (Jeremiah 44:17-19).
  • Their argument is empirical and exactly inverted: we prospered while we served her, and we have starved since we stopped (Jeremiah 44:17-18).
  • They have confused the timing. The famine came after Josiah's reform because of generations of guilt already accrued, not because the reform offended a goddess (Jeremiah 44:18).
  • The women's question in verse 19 is sharp and fair: did we do any of this without our husbands? The men had signed off on all of it (Jeremiah 44:19).
  • "Yahweh could no longer bear it" gives the limit in the plainest possible terms - patience that had a capacity, and reached it (Jeremiah 44:22).
  • "Establish then your vows, and perform your vows" is the most chilling sentence in the chapter: God stops arguing and hands them over to what they insisted on (Jeremiah 44:25).
  • The sentence in verse 26 is unique in Scripture - His name will no longer be spoken in the mouth of any man of Judah in Egypt. Not judgment by fire, but by silence (Jeremiah 44:26).
  • "I watch over them for evil, and not for good" turns the watching almond branch of chapter 1 around and points it at them (Jeremiah 44:27).
  • Verse 28 reduces the entire forty-year contest to a single question: "whose word will stand, mine or theirs" (Jeremiah 44:28).
  • The sign given is checkable and dated - Pharaoh Hophra handed to his enemies, which happened within about fifteen years (Jeremiah 44:30).

Jeremiah 44 - Opening Intro

This is the last sermon of Jeremiah's life that Scripture records, and it is the only one where the congregation answers back out loud. Nowhere else in the prophets does a whole assembly stand up and reply. What they say is: no.

Read verse 17 slowly, because it is one of the most honest arguments any idolater ever made, and it is entirely built out of data. When we burned incense to the queen of the sky, we had plenty of food, we were well, and we saw no evil. Since we stopped, we have lacked everything and been eaten by sword and famine. That is not superstition. That is a chart. They lived through Josiah's reform and then through the collapse, and they drew the obvious line between the two.

And they got it exactly backwards. The reform was the last mercy before a judgment that generations had already earned; the disasters that followed were the bill arriving, not the penalty for cleaning house. But you can see how it looked from inside. Correlation is a terrible pastor, and a whole nation ordered its worship around it.

The queen of the sky is Ishtar - Astarte - and the detail worth noticing is how homely her cult was. Cakes pressed in a mold with her face on them. Drink poured out at the door. This was not a state religion imposed from a palace; it was a family practice, run out of the kitchen, taught by mothers to daughters. Chapter 7 already showed us the whole household at it: children gathering wood, fathers lighting the fire, women kneading the dough. The most durable idolatry is almost always the domestic kind.

The women's reply in verse 19 deserves respect even in its defiance, because it is true. Did we make her cakes without our husbands? No. They did not. The men had known, and approved, and eaten. When Jeremiah answers he does not accept the division at all - he addresses the men, the women, and all the people together. There is no innocent party in the room.

Then comes verse 25, and God stops arguing. "Establish then your vows, and perform your vows." Go ahead. Do it. It is the sound of a door being left open on purpose, and it is far more frightening than any threat in the chapter. There is a judgment that consists simply of being permitted.

And the sentence in verse 26 has no parallel anywhere: My name will no longer be named in the mouth of any man of Judah in all the land of Egypt. They had used the phrase "as the Lord Yahweh lives" as a formality while serving another god - a signature on documents, a habit of speech. So the signature is revoked. The judgment is not that He shouts at them. It is that the sound of His name goes out of their mouths.

Before we read, listen for the question in verse 28, because it is the question the entire book has been asking: whose word will stand, mine or theirs? Everything else - the kings, the sieges, the false prophets, the burned scroll, the cistern - has been a long argument about that one sentence. And it is still the only question that matters about anything we are currently sure of.

CHAPTER 45 - A Word for Baruch

Key Notes

  • The oracle is dated to the fourth year of Jehoiakim - about 605 BC, some twenty years before the events of chapters 43 and 44 (Jeremiah 45:1).
  • Its placement here is deliberate. It closes the scroll the way a scribe signs his own work at the end of the manuscript (Jeremiah 45:1).
  • That fourth year is the year of chapter 36 - the scroll Baruch wrote, read aloud in the temple, and watched the king cut up and burn (Jeremiah 45:1).
  • Baruch is a man of standing. His brother Seraiah was Zedekiah's quartermaster, and a clay seal bearing his own name has been recovered - he had a career to lose (Jeremiah 45:2).
  • Verse 3 preserves the only words of Baruch recorded in Scripture, and they are a complaint: "Woe is me now!" (Jeremiah 45:3).
  • God quotes the complaint back to him verbatim before answering it. He had heard it exactly as it was said (Jeremiah 45:3).
  • "Yahweh has added sorrow to my pain" - Baruch does not blame circumstances or enemies. He says the grief came from God, and God does not correct him (Jeremiah 45:3).
  • The answer begins not with Baruch's trouble but with God's: what I built I am breaking down, what I planted I am plucking up (Jeremiah 45:4).
  • Those are the verbs of Jeremiah 1:10, and the demolition costs the Builder something. Baruch is being told his sorrow is a share in a larger one (Jeremiah 45:4).
  • "Do you seek great things for yourself? Don't seek them" is the only rebuke in the oracle, and it is a question rather than a verdict (Jeremiah 45:5).
  • The timing matters: greatness is not being called sinful, it is being called unavailable. This is not the era for it (Jeremiah 45:5).
  • The promise given is deliberately small and completely sufficient - "I will let you escape with your life wherever you go" (Jeremiah 45:5).
  • Life as spoil is this book's particular mercy, given to Ebed-Melech and to those who surrendered, and now to a scribe (Jeremiah 45:5).
  • "Wherever you go" is quietly generous, and it holds even in Egypt - which is where he ends up, carried off in chapter 43 (Jeremiah 45:5).

Jeremiah 45 - Opening Intro

Five verses, and they are for one man.

Baruch has been in this book since chapter 32, and he is almost always described by his function: the son of Neriah, who wrote at the mouth of Jeremiah. He buys the field deed and seals it. He takes dictation. He reads the scroll aloud in the temple when Jeremiah cannot go himself, and then hides while Jehoiakim slices it column by column into the brazier. In chapter 43 he is accused of running the prophet. In chapter 43 he is also carried into Egypt with him. He is the person in the room whose name is on nothing.

This is a man with prospects, too. His brother Seraiah served in Zedekiah's court, and archaeologists have recovered a clay bulla stamped "belonging to Berechiah son of Neriah the scribe." He came from the class that produced officials. Instead he spent his working life taking down the words of the most hated man in Jerusalem.

So in the fourth year of Jehoiakim, with his scroll in ashes and a warrant out for both of them, he says the one thing Scripture preserves from his mouth: "Woe is me now! For Yahweh has added sorrow to my pain! I am weary with my groaning, and I find no rest."

Notice three things about the answer he gets. First, God repeats his complaint back to him word for word before responding to it. Baruch had probably said it to himself, or to Jeremiah, on a bad night. It had been heard, precisely, and it is quoted with no mockery in it.

Second, the answer does not begin with Baruch. It begins with God: what I have built, I am breaking down; what I have planted, I am plucking up. Those are the verbs from Jeremiah's commission, and here they are turned on the Builder's own work. Before God says anything about Baruch's grief, He says: this is costing me too. Your sorrow is not being dismissed. It is being placed inside a larger one.

Third, the correction is a question, not a sentence. "Do you seek great things for yourself? Don't seek them." It is not that ambition is wicked. It is that this is not the season for it, and a man who keeps reaching for a career in a generation like this one will break his hands on it. The word for what he is offered instead is spoil - plunder, what a soldier walks off the field carrying. In a year when everything is being taken, your life will be the thing you get to keep.

It is a small promise. Read it in a comfortable century and it can sound like a consolation prize. Read it in 586, standing over the ruins with the temple still smoking, and it is enormous. And it comes with the phrase that must have mattered most twenty years later, when he was dragged across the Egyptian border by men who blamed him for the whole thing: "wherever you go."

Before we read, take the smallness of this seriously. The God who was in the middle of dismantling a nation stopped, in the fourth year of Jehoiakim, to answer one exhausted man who had not asked for anything except to be allowed to feel it. He never told him the pain was unwarranted. He told him it was shared, and that he would come out of it alive.

Major Themes Across Jeremiah 43-45

  • Denying the commission when the message cannot be answered - "Yahweh has not sent you"
  • Blaming the scribe: how a word gets dismissed by finding someone to blame for it
  • Fear rewriting a promise into a threat, and then arguing with its own version
  • A homecoming undone by the people who survived to enjoy it
  • God still speaking on the wrong side of a border - disobedience changes the address, not the relationship
  • Prophecy done with the hands: stones in wet mortar at a foreign king's door
  • Empires as instruments - "Nebuchadnezzar my servant" - and the gods of Egypt worn like a coat
  • Idolatry as self-harm: great evil committed against our own souls
  • Correlation mistaken for covenant, and a nation's worship ordered around the years that went well
  • The domestic idol, taught in kitchens and passed down, harder to remove than any state religion
  • Being handed over to what we insisted on - "perform your vows" as a sentence, not a permission
  • A name withdrawn from the mouth that used it as a formality
  • The one question underneath the whole book: whose word will stand, mine or theirs
  • A tired servant heard by name, his complaint quoted back without mockery
  • Ambition set down for a season, and life itself received as spoil

Let this be the word that goes before us tonight:

May we never call a word false simply because it asks us to stay somewhere hard.

May we stop looking for the Baruch in the room - the influence, the agenda, the handler - when what we have actually been handed is the word of God.

May we hear what He said, and not the frightened version of it we composed on the way home.

May He keep us from crossing borders He did not open, and if we have already crossed one, may we remember that He has followed us there and is still speaking.

May we be honest about the years we credit to the wrong god, and brave enough to keep obeying through the seasons that look like they are costing us.

May the idols we inherited in our own kitchens be named and put down in our generation, so that our children are not taught them by our hands.

May He never say to us, perform your vows - and may we never be so certain that He stops arguing with us.

May His name stay in our mouths, and mean what it says when it is there.

And for those of us who are tired tonight, who have carried someone else's work and found no rest in it - may we hear Him quote our own complaint back to us gently, and promise us our lives wherever we go.

May every promise He has spoken over us stand up, come forth, and manifest in its appointed time.

Amen.
