Tonight Yahweh walks down the hill to the palace and audits the throne - and then, having found every occupant of it wanting, He promises a King who will not be.

A cedar-panelled house built with wages never paid.

A signet ring pulled off the right hand that wore it.

A righteous Branch raised up to David, whose name is Yahweh Our Righteousness.

Two baskets of figs on the temple steps - one good, one so rotten no one would touch it - and the shock of finding out which basket is which.

Jeremiah 22 works through three of Josiah's descendants by name and asks the question that undoes them all: your father did justice and it was well with him - wasn't that what it means to know me?

Jeremiah 23 turns on the shepherds who scattered the flock and the prophets who ran without being sent, and in the middle of that indictment sets the clearest messianic promise in the book so far.

Jeremiah 24 upends every assumption about who is blessed, by declaring that the ones already carried off to Babylon are the good figs.

This is not just history.

This is a mirror.

And tonight, we let the Word fill us.

CHAPTER 22 - Cedar Palaces and the Signet Plucked Off

Key Notes

  • Jeremiah is sent down to the palace itself - not to summon the king to the temple, but to stand in his house and address him where he governs (Jeremiah 22:1-2).
  • The requirement laid on the throne is entirely ethical and entirely concrete: justice and righteousness, the robbed delivered, no violence to the foreigner, the fatherless, or the widow, no innocent blood (Jeremiah 22:3).
  • The Davidic promise is presented as conditional in practice. Do this, and kings keep riding through these gates; refuse, and Yahweh swears by Himself that the house becomes a ruin (Jeremiah 22:4-5).
  • The palace is addressed as a forest - Gilead, the head of Lebanon - because it was panelled in imported cedar. Yahweh promises woodsmen with axes (Jeremiah 22:6-7).
  • The nations will ask why, and the answer given is not military but covenantal: they abandoned the covenant of Yahweh their God and served other gods (Jeremiah 22:8-9).
  • Shallum - the king 2 Kings calls Jehoahaz - reigned three months and died in Egypt. Weep for the exiled living rather than the buried dead (Jeremiah 22:10-12).
  • The woe against Jehoiakim is about labor practices: a wide house with spacious rooms and painted cedar, built by neighbors who were never paid their wages (Jeremiah 22:13-14).
  • Then the sentence that indicts every religious generation: Josiah did justice and judged the cause of the poor and needy - "wasn't this to know me?" Knowing Yahweh is defined as behavior toward the vulnerable (Jeremiah 22:15-16).
  • Jehoiakim will be buried with the burial of a donkey, dragged out beyond the gates - no lament, no "Ah, my brother," no royal mourning at all (Jeremiah 22:18-19).
  • Coniah - Jehoiachin - is called the signet on Yahweh's right hand, the most intimate image of authority available, and is then plucked off it and thrown into a land he was not born in (Jeremiah 22:24-27).
  • The chapter ends with a triple cry, "O earth, earth, earth," and a verdict recording Coniah as childless: no descendant of his will prosper on David's throne (Jeremiah 22:28-30).

Jeremiah 22 - Opening Intro

This chapter is a royal audit, and the standard it audits by is not orthodoxy. Yahweh does not ask the kings of Judah about their theology, their temple attendance, or their liturgical reforms. He asks what happened to the widow, the orphan, the foreigner, and the man whose wages were withheld.

Verse 16 is the hinge, and it is worth reading twice. Yahweh describes Josiah - ate and drank, did justice and righteousness, judged the cause of the poor and needy - and then asks, "Wasn't this to know me?" Not "this proved he knew me." This *was* knowing Him. The doing and the knowing are the same act.

Set against that, Jehoiakim's crime is almost mundane. He wanted a nicer house. Spacious rooms, cut windows, cedar ceiling, painted vermilion - and he built it with unpaid labor. There is no idol mentioned in his woe. Just a construction project and a payroll he never met.

Then comes Coniah, and the image Yahweh uses is startling in its intimacy. A signet ring is how an ancient king signed his name; it never left his hand. Yahweh says: even if he were the signet on my right hand, I would pull him off. And He does.

One thread to hold as you read, because it becomes important next chapter: verse 30 records Coniah as childless and says no offspring of his will prosper on David's throne. That looks like the end of the promise. Matthew's genealogy runs Messiah's legal line straight through Jeconiah, while Luke's traces a different branch of David's house entirely - which is one reason many readers see the two genealogies as answering exactly this verse.

Before we read, let the standard land where it lands. If knowing God is measured by how the powerless in our reach are treated, then most of us have been measuring ourselves with the wrong instrument.

CHAPTER 23 - The Righteous Branch and the Prophets Who Ran Unsent

Key Notes

  • The woe falls on the shepherds - the same kings just audited - for scattering the flock and failing to visit them. Yahweh's counter-threat is exact: "I will visit on you" (Jeremiah 23:1-2).
  • Yahweh then takes the shepherd's job Himself: "I will gather the remnant of my flock out of all the countries where I have driven them," and will set up shepherds who actually feed (Jeremiah 23:3-4).
  • The promise arrives in the language of horticulture, not politics: a righteous Branch raised up to David, who will reign and deal wisely and execute justice - the very thing the last four kings would not do (Jeremiah 23:5).
  • His name is the whole gospel in four words: "Yahweh our righteousness." Not a king who enforces righteousness on others, but one who *is* righteousness for the people He rules (Jeremiah 23:6).
  • As in chapter 16, the coming deliverance will retire the exodus as Israel's defining oath - the new testimony will be the gathering out of the north country (Jeremiah 23:7-8).
  • Jeremiah's own reaction to the oracles against the prophets is physical: a broken heart, shaking bones, the disorientation of a drunk man - "because of Yahweh, and because of his holy words" (Jeremiah 23:9).
  • The corruption has reached the sanctuary: "both prophet and priest are profane. Yes, in my house I have found their wickedness" (Jeremiah 23:10-11).
  • The false prophets' message is diagnosed precisely - they promise peace to those who despise Yahweh and safety to those walking in the stubbornness of their own hearts (Jeremiah 23:16-17).
  • The test is stated as a question about proximity: who has stood in the council of Yahweh? Had they stood there, they would have turned the people from evil instead of soothing them (Jeremiah 23:18-22).
  • "Am I a God at hand, and not a God afar off?" answers the theology behind false prophecy - a local, manageable deity who fills a shrine rather than heaven and earth (Jeremiah 23:23-24).
  • The dream-peddlers are contrasted with the faithful: "What is the straw to the wheat?" And the word itself is described as fire and as a hammer that breaks rock (Jeremiah 23:25-29).
  • The chapter ends by banning a phrase. They had turned "the burden of Yahweh" into a joke, so Yahweh takes the expression away from them entirely (Jeremiah 23:33-40).

Jeremiah 23 - Opening Intro

Notice how this chapter is built. Yahweh dismantles the shepherds, and then - before He says a word about replacements - He says what He Himself will do. "I will gather." "I will bring them again." "I will set up shepherds." The failure of leadership is real, and it is not the last word about the flock.

Then comes verse 5, and the metaphor is deliberate. Judah's royal house had just been described as a cedar forest marked for the axe. Now Yahweh promises a tsemach - a shoot, a sprout, the green thing that comes up out of a stump after the tree has been cut down. The dynasty is going to be felled. Something is going to grow anyway.

And His name stops you: Yahweh Tsidkenu, Yahweh Our Righteousness. Every king in chapter 22 was measured by whether he *did* righteousness. This One is called righteousness - not the enforcer of a standard but the supply of it for the people He governs. Chapter 22 asks what a righteous king owes his people; chapter 23 answers that the King will be their righteousness.

The rest of the chapter is about how to tell a true word from a comfortable one, and Yahweh's test in verse 18 is not about accuracy or eloquence. It is about location. Has this man stood in my council? Because if he had, he would have come back saying something that turned people from evil, not something that let them stay where they were.

There is a Hebrew wordplay in the closing section that the English cannot carry. The word massa means both "oracle" and "burden." The people had begun sneering, "What's the burden of Yahweh today?" - and Yahweh answers by taking the word off their tongues: you may not use that phrase anymore.

Before we read, hold verse 29 next to verse 17. One kind of preaching tells people what they wish were true. The other is fire and a hammer that breaks rock. Both feel like ministry from the inside. Only one of them has been anywhere near the council of Yahweh.

CHAPTER 24 - Two Baskets of Figs and a Heart to Know Him

Key Notes

  • The vision is dated: after Nebuchadnezzar carried off Jeconiah, the princes, the craftsmen and the smiths. This is the deportation of 597 BC, and Jerusalem is still standing (Jeremiah 24:1).
  • Two baskets sit in front of the temple. One holds figs like the first-ripe - the earliest, sweetest harvest. The other holds figs too rotten to eat (Jeremiah 24:2).
  • Yahweh asks the prophetic question He asks throughout Scripture - "What do you see, Jeremiah?" - and waits for the man to say it out loud before explaining it (Jeremiah 24:3).
  • Then the shock. The good figs are the exiles. The people everyone in Jerusalem regarded as the casualties of divine anger are the ones Yahweh regards "for good" (Jeremiah 24:5).
  • "I will set my eyes on them for good" reverses the language of judgment used earlier in the book, where His eyes were on all their ways for evil (Jeremiah 24:6).
  • The four verbs of Jeremiah's original call return, and this time only the constructive two are used: "I will build them, and not pull them down. I will plant them, and not pluck them up" (Jeremiah 24:6).
  • Verse 7 is the theological center: "I will give them a heart to know me." The knowing is not achieved by the exiles - it is given to them, and the returning follows from the gift (Jeremiah 24:7).
  • The covenant formula appears in full - "They will be my people, and I will be their God" - inside a chapter about deportees rather than temple worshipers (Jeremiah 24:7).
  • The bad figs are Zedekiah, his princes, the remnant left in Jerusalem, and those who fled to Egypt: everyone still living in the promised land and still congratulating themselves for it (Jeremiah 24:8).
  • Their sentence is dispersal without purpose - a reproach, a proverb, a taunt, and a curse in every place they are driven, until they are consumed off the land (Jeremiah 24:9-10).

Jeremiah 24 - Opening Intro

Everything about this chapter depends on knowing who was in Jerusalem when it was given.

In 597 BC Nebuchadnezzar took the young king Jeconiah, the nobles, and every skilled craftsman and metalworker in the city, and marched them to Babylon. The people left behind drew the obvious conclusion. Those exiles must have been the wicked ones. We are the ones God spared. The temple is still standing and we are still standing in it.

So Yahweh sets two baskets of figs in front of them, and asks Jeremiah to say what he sees, and then tells him which basket is which. The good figs - the early-ripe ones, the kind you eat first because they are the best of the harvest - are the deportees. The rotten figs are the congregation still attending services in Jerusalem.

That should unsettle anyone who has ever read their own circumstances as a scorecard. Being in the land was not proof of favor. Being in Babylon was not proof of rejection. Yahweh says of the exiles, "I will set my eyes on them for good" - and He says it while they are seven hundred miles from the temple with nothing left of their religion but a memory.

Then verse 7, which is the quiet mountain in this chapter: "I will give them a heart to know me." Not I will command them to know me. Not I will wait for them to decide to know me. I will give them the organ with which knowing happens. And notice the order - the heart is given first, and the returning with the whole heart follows from it. Grace is upstream of repentance here, and Jeremiah says so nineteen chapters before he says it again in chapter 31.

Before we read, hold this gently: the losses that looked like judgment may be the very furnace where a heart to know Him is being formed. The people with everything intact were the ones in trouble.

Major Themes Across Jeremiah 22-24

  • Knowing Yahweh defined as doing justice - the poor and needy as the instrument that measures a heart
  • Power audited by how it treats the powerless, with unpaid wages weighed as heavily as idolatry
  • The Davidic promise held and threatened at once: kings plucked off like a signet, the line never ended
  • A stump felled and a Branch raised - Yahweh growing something out of what He Himself cut down
  • Yahweh Our Righteousness: a King who supplies righteousness rather than merely demanding it
  • Yahweh taking up the shepherd's work Himself when every appointed shepherd has scattered the flock
  • The test of a true word - not eloquence or accuracy, but whether the speaker has stood in His council
  • A God who fills heaven and earth against a God kept conveniently local and manageable
  • Circumstances read wrongly: exile as mercy, an intact life as judgment, and the baskets reversed
  • A heart to know Him given as a gift, with wholehearted return as the fruit of the gift and not its price

Benediction

Let this be the word that goes before us tonight:

May the God who asked a king whether doing justice was not the same as knowing Him teach us that our theology is only as sound as our treatment of the people who cannot repay us.

May He keep us from building anything beautiful on wages we never paid, and may nothing we own be worth what it cost someone else.

May He be to us not a manageable God at hand but the One who fills heaven and earth, and may we stop trying to shrink Him to the size of our preferences.

May He raise up in us a hunger for the word that is fire and hammer, and may we grow suspicious of every comfort that has never been anywhere near His council.

May the Branch reign over us - Yahweh our righteousness - and may we stop trying to be our own, since He has already been made ours.

May He gather what has been scattered, and set His eyes on us for good, and build and plant where He once had to pull down and pluck up.

And when our circumstances look like rejection, may we remember the baskets, and trust that the furnace we are in may be exactly where He is forming a heart that knows Him.

May every promise He has spoken over us stand up, come forth, and manifest in its appointed time.

Amen.
